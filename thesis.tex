\section{Introduction}
Question: To what extent does Neural Posterior Estimation improve the calibration and macro economical forecasting performance of a macroeconomic Agent-Based Model relative to traditional estimation methods?

In the current state of the art macro economic modeling, there are two main  
approaches. The first one is based on Dynamic Stochastic General Equilibrium (DSGE) models, which are widely used in macroeconomics for policy analysis and forecasting. Popularized by Smets \& Wouters (2007), these models are built on microeconomic foundations and incorporate rational expectations. However, they often rely on strong assumptions and may not capture the complexity of real-world economies. 

The second approach, analyzed in this paper, is based on Agent-Based Models (ABMs). First used for economic modelling by Orcutt (1957), ABMs have gained popularity after the financial crisis of 2008. Presenting a more flexible framework than DSGE models, ABMs allow for the inclusion of heterogeneous agents, non-linear interactions, and emergent phenomena. Results by Farmer and Foley (2009), suggest that ABMs can provide valuable insights into economic dynamics and policy implications, especially in situations where traditional models struggle to capture the complexity of the economy. While being a promising approach, they have several weakness. Due to the bottom up design choice, ABM's often have many parameters, for which a calibration procedure is required. 

In the beginning, researchers used point - wise calibration methods, but with growing interest in Bayesian methods and increased computational power of current computers, more and more studies resort to using approximate Bayesian procedures for parameter identification. This has been made possible by continuous development of such methods. From approximate bayesian computation (ABC), likelihood function approximations, to current state of the art approaches based on neural networks (NN) such as: neural posterior / ratio / likelihood estimation techniques (NPE, NRE, NLE respectively). Following Dyer et al. (2024) we focus on NPE and NRE approaches for parameter identification in cutting - edge macro economical ABM developed by Poledna et al. (2023). 

Their model is the first ABM that can rival AR, VAR, VECM, DSGE models in out - sample forecasting and has already been extended by Wiese et al. (2024) who introduced e.g. housing market and multiple country calibration. They followed the Bayesian inference trend and used SBI techniques to identify behavioral parameters influencing firms demand predictions and production targets. We continue with SBI techniques by investigating identification of parameters in the matching procedure and inflation pass through for import prices in the aforementioned baseline ABM calibrated to the Italian economy. We  train NPE and NRE estimators on simulated hypothetical trajectories of five economical aggregates: real GDP, inflation, investment, household and government expenditure, all computed by the model, to answer the question, whether those SBI techniques can identify these parameters based on the given observables. The resulting posteriors are checked for identification using sweep coverage checks and for validation using Simulation Based Calibration (SBC; Talts et al. 2018) supplemented by Posterior Predictive Checks. Moreover, we check whether the computed posteriors can enhance forecasting performance of the model for the observables. The following chapters are organized as follows, 2nd gives on overview of macroenomical forecasting with ABM and introduces the SBI methods with diagnostics. Chapter 3 presents parameter and prior selection, implementation details, NPE and NRE algorithms, validation and forecasting approaches. The results are all methods are presented in chapter 4, followed by conclusions in chapter 5.

\section{Literature review}
The development of modern macroeconomic modelling progressed from early Keynesian aggregate models to microfounded dynamic frameworks. Following the critique of policy evaluation advanced by Robert E. Lucas Jr. (1976), macroeconomics increasingly adopted models grounded in optimizing individual behavior. This evolution culminated in Dynamic Stochastic General Equilibrium (DSGE) models, which combine intertemporal optimization, rational expectations, and stochastic shocks within a general equilibrium framework. Real Business Cycle models (Kydland \& Prescott, 1982) established the foundations of this approach, while later New Keynesian DSGE models incorporated nominal rigidities and additional frictions to improve empirical performance (Smets \& Wouters, 2003). Despite their widespread use in academia and central banks, DSGE models have been criticized for their reliance on representative agents and strong rationality assumptions, which may limit their ability to capture heterogeneity, financial instability, and emergent macroeconomic phenomena (Kirman, 1992).

Agent-Based Models (ABMs) emerged as an alternative computational paradigm that explicitly represents economies as systems of interacting heterogeneous agents. Drawing inspiration from complexity science and computational social science, ABMs dispense with the requirement of equilibrium and instead model aggregate outcomes as emergent properties of decentralized interactions. Early contributions such as Growing Artificial Societies (Epstein \& Axtell, 1996) demonstrated how simple behavioral rules could generate complex macro-level patterns. During the 1990s and early 2000s, ABMs gained traction through the work of researchers such as Arthur (1994), LeBaron (2006), and Tesfatsion (2006), who argued that bounded rationality, learning, and network interactions provide a more realistic representation of economic behavior than fully rational representative agents. As computational resources expanded, ABMs evolved from stylized theoretical exercises into empirically grounded tools capable of reproducing key macroeconomic regularities, including business cycles, firm-size distributions, unemployment dynamics, and financial crises.

The application of ABMs to macroeconomics accelerated after the 2008 global financial crisis, which exposed limitations of equilibrium-based models in capturing systemic risk and endogenous instability. Large-scale macroeconomic ABMs, such as the Eurace and Mark models, introduced detailed representations of households, firms, banks, and labor markets, enabling researchers to study the transmission of shocks through complex economic networks (Dawid et al., 2012; Dosi et al., 2010). These models demonstrated that macroeconomic fluctuations can emerge endogenously from agent interactions rather than being driven solely by exogenous shocks. Consequently, ABMs have become increasingly important for policy analysis, stress testing, and the study of non-linear economic dynamics that are difficult to accommodate within conventional DSGE frameworks.
A persistent challenge in agent-based macroeconomic modelling concerns empirical calibration and validation. Because ABMs generally lack tractable likelihood functions and often contain large numbers of interacting parameters, obtaining reliable parameter estimates for forecasting applications is substantially more difficult than in conventional econometric models. Early calibration approaches relied on manual tuning, grid search, and optimization-based procedures. Among the most influential methods, the Simulated Method of Moments (SMM) and related Simulated Minimum Distance (SMD) techniques calibrate model parameters by minimizing the discrepancy between simulated and empirical summary statistics (Lee \& Ingram, 1991; Grazzini \& Richiardi, 2013). More generally, these approaches produce a point estimate of the parameter vector, such that ($\hat{\theta} = \arg\min_{\theta \in \Theta} f(y,\theta)$), where (y) denotes the observed data and ($f(\cdot)$) is a suitable distance or loss function. While computationally feasible, point-estimation methods do not directly quantify parameter uncertainty and may be sensitive to the choice of calibration targets.

To address these limitations, recent research has increasingly adopted Bayesian approaches to ABM calibration. Rather than producing a single estimate, Bayesian methods infer the posterior distribution of model parameters conditional on the observed data, thereby explicitly accounting for uncertainty. However, Bayesian inference traditionally requires evaluation of the likelihood function ($p(x \mid \theta)$), which is typically unavailable for large-scale ABMs due to their complex and highly nonlinear dynamics (Dyer et al., 2024). Grazzini et al. (2017) addressed this challenge by introducing several Approximate Bayesian Computation (ABC) approaches for ABM calibration. These methods either approximate the likelihood function directly through parametric or non-parametric techniques, such as kernel density estimation combined with Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling, or bypass likelihood evaluation altogether through likelihood-free posterior estimation. Despite methodological differences, all ABC approaches rely on repeated simulations from the underlying model and therefore belong to the broader class of Simulation-Based Inference (SBI) methods. Importantly, Grazzini et al. (2017) demonstrated that posterior estimation can provide richer information than traditional calibration approaches by quantifying uncertainty around parameter estimates rather than producing only a single optimal value.

Subsequent work extended these ideas in several directions. Platt (2022) proposed neural-network-based approximations of model likelihoods, while Lux (2018, 2022) developed Monte Carlo approaches based on particle filtering techniques. In parallel, advances in machine learning led to the development of neural likelihood estimation methods such as Sequential Neural Likelihood (SNL) (Papamakarios et al., 2019). Although these approaches improved the scalability of likelihood-free inference, they often require extremely large simulation budgets—ranging from approximately ($10^5$) simulations for relatively low-dimensional problems to ($10^7$) or more for higher-dimensional parameter spaces—and commonly rely on assumptions such as simulator stationarity (Dyer et al., 2024). To overcome these limitations, Dyer et al. (2024) advocate discriminative SBI methods based on Neural Posterior Estimation (NPE) (Papamakarios \& Murray, 2016; Lueckmann et al., 2017; Greenberg et al., 2019) and Neural Ratio Estimation (NRE) (Hermans et al., 2020; Thomas et al. 2022, Miller et al. 2024), which seek to improve the statistical and computational efficiency of Bayesian calibration for complex agent-based models.

NPE approximates the posterior ($p(\theta \mid x)$) directly using a trainable Naural Network (NN): $q_\phi(\theta \mid x)$. It development started with the work by Papamakarios \& Murray (2016) who introduced the first NPE (NPE\_A) algorithm as an parametric alternative to ABC approaches. It was based on parametrized gaussian MDN (Bishop 1994) and allowed for sequential training. Sequential training is based on splitting the simulation budget into $R$ sets: $N_1, \dots, N_R$ and iteratively updating the target approximation based on previous posterior estimates. It might be useful if the inference is based on a single observed data point. In NPE\_A importance weighting transformation was used to account for change of sampling distribution. This restricted the conditional density estimator to have a trackable probability function $q(\theta \mid x)$. Lueckmann et al. (2017) proposed (S)NPE\_B, which incorporated importance weighting directly into the training objective and introduced a calibration kernel to focus inference on simulations close to the observed data, while also allowing for more flexible prior and proposal distributions. However, this approach can suffer from increased estimator variance. A solution was introduced by Greenberg et al. (2019). Who were the first to use Normalizing Flows (NF) (Tabak \& Vanden-Eijnden, 2010; Tabak and Turner, 2013; Rezende and Mohamed, 2015) as the NN architecture and addressed limitations of (S)NPE\_B by introducing Automatic Posterior Transformation (APT), and atomic proposals. Their method ((S)NPE\_C) enabled flexible priors and proposals while maintaining stable estimation. By learning a transformed posterior that serves both as the posterior approximation and proposal distribution, (S)NPE\_C avoids analytical transformations between rounds and has been shown to be more robust and flexible than earlier (S)NPE variants, with performance comparable to or exceeding that of Sequential Neural Likelihood (SNL). However, as noted in the original APT paper (Greenberg et al. 2019) and by Durkan et al. (2020), this method can accumulate the posterior mass outside of prior bounds (what is referred as 'leakage') and can make rejection sampling time consuming (e.g. Glöckler et al. 2022). This issue was addressed by Deistler et al. (2022) with the introduction of automatic threshold based truncation of the posteriors (TSNPE). Departing from sequential developments, Dax et al. (2023) build upon the NF architecture by incorporating Flow Matching technique (Lipman et al. 2022) which uses continuous transformation instead of fixed (discrete) number of function as in standard NF.

A related line of work focuses on Neural Ratio Estimation (NRE), which approximates the likelihood-to-evidence ratio and recovers the posterior through Bayes’ rule. As with NPE, both amortized and sequential variants of NRE have been developed. Early approaches formulated ratio estimation as a binary classification problem trained on simulated data (Hermans et al., 2020; Thomas et al., 2021). Durkan et al. (2020) later introduced NRE\_B, which replaced the binary objective with a contrastive classification framework, improving sample efficiency and reducing gradient variance. However, this modification sacrifices exact identification of the likelihood-to-evidence ratio, potentially introducing bias in ratio estimates. To address this limitation, Miller et al. (2024) proposed NRE\_C, which modifies the contrastive objective to restore exact ratio identification while retaining the efficiency gains of NRE\_B. Consequently, the development of NRE mirrors that of NPE: successive methodological refinements have sought to improve simulation efficiency, estimator stability, and flexibility while preserving accurate posterior inference.  

Simulation based methods rely on outputs from some complex, stochastic models. Especially for ABM, resulting vector is a high dimensional, possibly multi featured, time series, thus it can be beneficial to lower the input dimension for the NPE network (e.g. Dyer et al. 2024) or incorporate some prior knowledge about output (e.g. exchangeability, dependence) as in time series. This can be done in two main approaches: selecting a set of summary statistics that describe the output, using a NN to learn informative features from data directly. The first is especially relevant for classical ABC approaches, where the algorithm mainly depends on the choice of distance function and summary statistics (e.g. Greenberg et al. 2019). Thus some literature exists for choosing and testing summary statistics (e.g. Prangle 2018). However, this method can lead to, hard to quantify, losses of information (e.g. Radev et al. 2020).

The second approach relies on constructing an auxiliary NN that maps simulation output $x$ to a fixed size vector of learned statistics: $s_l(x)$. It is trained together with the density network from the same loss propagation. Many different choices exist for this network, from simple MLP for finance models (Stilmann et al. 2023), 1D CNN (Radev et al. 2020), standard CNN used for images (Greenber et al. 2019) or recurrent neural networks (Gers et al. 2000; Cho et al. 2014; Dyer et al. 2024) first used by Greenberg et al. (2017) for time series inputs. Another approach was introduced by Redev et al. (2020) with BayesFlow architecture, which is based on invertible neural networks needed for iid. simulation output.

After computing the approximate joint posterior distribution using NPE or a similar methods, one would like to verify whether the result can be trustworthy. The first easy step, is to construct a pairplot, conditioning on the known data ($x_\text{obs}$). For a correct inference we would like to see a narrow posterior, with mass collapsed onto smaller region of the prior space. One can extend this method to check whether parameters from the posterior give rise to sensible simulation result - close to observed data. (named: Posterior Predictive Checks). While both are useful, they do not provide any mathematical notion of fit. One of the first formal tests was introduced by Geweke (2004). Author suggests to check whether the posterior sampler (aka. approximation) reconstructs the joint distribution by constructing a Gibbs sampler from the estimated posterior: $q(\theta |x )$ and model's likelihood function: $p(x \mid \theta)$. If the inference is correct, then the sampler will produce accurate joint samples indistinguishable from those generated directly from the prior: $p(\theta, x) = p(x \mid \theta)p(\theta)$ (we can sample $p(x, \theta)$ both components are known). Z scores can be used to perform a formal test. However, as Talts et al. (2018) note, the sampler can take a while to converge and validity of z - scores rely on this property.

Cook et al. (2006) introduces another crucial idea, instead of comparing summaries of the joint distribution using z - scores, we check whether the true value behaves as a draw from its own posterior. This novel approach uses rank statistics (estimated quantiles) to perform a formal check dimension wise. If model and posterior is correct, theses statistics should have a uniform distribution. Authors suggest to formally check this by transforming resulting quantiles into z - scores by the inverse normal CDF function. Moreover, the shape of the resulting histogram can give a hint about the source of miss specification. While correct on some experiments, Talts et al. shows that this procedure can inhibit bias when using MCMC sampling. To address this issue, they introduce Simulation Based Calibration (SBC), which is also based on rank statistics, but do now assume independent draws from the posterior sampler. Instead of proving the result for quantiles (continuous uniform), they show that the rank statistics for one dimensional variable follow a discrete uniform distribution. 

Another approach is based on computing empirical expected coverage statistics. Used first by Prangle et al. 2013 in the context of SBI tasks. At the basis, coverage tests ask a different question than SBC. Instead of computing rank statistics, they sample from the posterior conditioning on some $x$ generated from a ground truth parameter: $\tilde{\theta}$ and check how often does it fall inside the credible interval computed form the sample. This method allows to measure uncertainty in the approximated posterior. It provides a necessary but not a sufficient check (Lemos et al. 2023) and can be difficult to approximate practically (Deistler et al. 2022). For low dimensional parameter space one can use grid evaluation to approximate the resultant integral, but for larger space a MCMC sampler is necessary, what can be computationally expensive. Addressing this issue, Lemos et al. (2023) introduce a novel approach to coverage tests named TARP. It relies on testing whether posterior credible regions achieve their advertised coverage. They show that TARP provides a necessary and sufficient condition, while scaling better than SBC for high - dimensional parameter vectors. 

More diagnostic methods has been proposed for SBI inference procedures. For NPE based on Normalizing Flows (NF) Linhart et al. (2022) introduced global and local consistency checks (also see Zhao et al. 2021) based on probability integral transform (PIT). Recent studies push local diagnostic tests further. Dey et al. (2025) introduces LADaR, which uses discrepancies between empirical and theoretical CDFs to update the conditional density approximation and Linhart et al. (2023) extends the classifier two sample test (C2ST, see Lopez - Paz \& Oquab 2016) to local evaluation of the posterior ($l$-C2ST). However, as Laplante et al. (2026) note these newer diagnostics method aren't that common in SBI workflows. Moreover, since SBI by definition a Bayesian approach, many methods exists for model selection, which can be applied to compare the baseline model to the parametrized one or compare posterior estimation methods themselves. The main tool for this problem is Bayes factor (see e.g. Kass and Raftery 1995) which compares two model's marginal likelihood. Instead of pairwise comparisons (which does not yield correct test's power for multiple comparisons) we can either perform hierarchical Bayesian inference with some priors for the models, and choose one with highest posterior probability (Posterior Model Propabilities, see e.g. Gelman et al. 2013), use information criteria such as DIC (Spiegelhalter et al. (2002)) and WAIC (Watanabe 2010) to select one out of many models or alternatively perform Cross Validation methods such as: PSI-LOO (see e.g. Jung \& Templin 2026). However, since the main inquiry of this paper is about NPE, NRE parameter recovery, we mainly focus on standard SBI techniques for posterior validation such as expected coverage sweeps and SBC.

While new SBI techniques were being developed and inference procedure checks, not much research was devoted to robustness analysis, especially under model misspecification. Hermans et al.(2021) introduce the concepts of conservative and overconfident estimators based on coverage diagnostics and show that all benchmarked approaches provide non - conservative posteriors. Authors note that this issue is especially prominent with sequential methods with small training size per round and that SBI methods can be unreliable with difficult to detect issues. Their work was complemented by Cannon et al. (2022) where authors investigated SBI behaviour for misspecified models, meaning that simulator's induced $p(x \mid \theta)$ does not correspond to the true generating process of observed data $y$ for any parameter values. They found that SBI techniques fail catastrophically, producing overconfident posteriors, if the discrepancy between the model and real data is significant. Moreover, this issue is especially prominent for sequential methods with small round sizes. To address these issues, improvements have been proposed to introduce robust training for NPE and NRE. Staring from Robust NPE (RNPE) developed by Ward et al. (2022) which incorporates an error model to account for discrepancies between model's induced and real data likelihoods. Their methods was improved by Huang et al. (2023) by NPE with Robust Statistics (NPE-RS) which used Mean Minimum Distance (MMD) (Gretton et al. 2012) to regularize standard NPE loss. For ratio estimation, Delaunoy et al. (2022) introduced Balanced NRE (BNRE) by restricting model hypothesis space to balanced classifiers, which are less confident than optimal Bayes classifier. Authors show that their methods produces conservative posteriors for simulation budgets, addressing the issues raised by Hermans et al. (2021).


With these new diagnostic approaches and constant improvements to the inference procedures.: such as DRO-NPE (Laplante et al. 2026). Neural SBI techniques improve inference, especially for smaller sized training datasets. Wiese et al. (2024) used this approach to estimate behavioral parameters in extended model based on work by Poledna et al. (2023) while Alves et al. (2026) used NPE to identify parameters of an labor market ABM. We follow this trend and adopt these techniques to identify parameters related to matching procedure and import pricing in the baseline ABM model developed by Poledna et al. (2026).

\section{Methodology}
Our analysis is based on applying SBI techniques for parameters estimation / identification in ABM models. We used a macro economical model developed by Poledna et al. (2023) with a few minor changes to allow changes in the chosen parameters. When running the required simulations we replace the original hard coded values for the parameters with draws from the priors or posteriors. Since analysis is based on posterior estimation and forecasting performance, thee main steps can be identified:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Posterior estimation using NPE and NRE method on synthetical time series data from 2013Q1 to 2020Q4. We use the model to generate those trajectories.
    \item Validation of the posterior using median sweeps and Simulation Based Calibration (SBC) developed by Talts et al. (2018).
    \item Evaluation of forecasting performance of the model with parameters obtained from NPE and NRE.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Posterior Estimation}

\subsubsection*{Parameters}

In the original model, most parameters are identified from available data. Sources include government statistics, input - output tables, sector accounts and demography data. However, there is a number of parameters that are hard coded in the original model, but can differ between countries and correspond to the matching procedure and trade channel. Thus, it might be difficult to develop a standard calibration procedure and likelihood free techniques seems like a perfect fit. 

We follow the approach by Wiese et al. (2024) and Dyer et al. (2024) to use SBI techniques to compute posteriors for those parameters. They used NPE and NRE to estimate four 4 parameters influencing firms target production. We investigate a different aspect of the model by parameterizing two computation during goods-market matching step inside the model by introducing parameters: $\omega$ and $\lambda_p$ and relative pricing of imported goods: $\bar{\pi}$. In the matching step, each agent who need to fulfill their demands visit a number of firms. The probability a firm will be visited depends on (1) product price via an exponential distribution and (2) the relative size of the firm, such that larger companies have greater chance of being visited. The two are then combined by averaging. The parameters we introduce change (1) how strongly agents favor cheaper products ($\lambda_p$) and (2) how the two components are averaged ($\omega$). We think that the chosen parameters depend on the considered economy, while lack a clear point-wise way of calibration. What makes SBI approach perfect fit. The original computation for probabilities in the market matching process in Poledna et al. (2023) is as follows:
\begin{align*}
    pr_i^{\text{price}}(t) &= \frac{e^{-2P_i(t)}}{\sum_{i \in I_{s = g}}e^{-2P_i(t)}} \\
    pr_i^{\text{size}}(t) &= \frac{Y_i(t)}{\sum_{i \in I_{s = g}}Y_i(t)} \\
    pr_i^{\text{cum}}(t) &= \frac{pr_i^\text{price} + pr_i^\text{size}}{2}
\end{align*}

We parametrize the soft max computation by $\lambda_p$ and final weighting by $\omega$:
\begin{align*} 
    pr_i^{\text{price}}(t) &= \frac{e^{- \lambda_p P_i(t)}}{\sum_{i \in I_{s = g}}e^{-\lambda_p P_i(t)}} \\
    pr_i^{\text{size}}(t) &= \frac{Y_i(t)}{\sum_{i \in I_{s = g}}Y_i(t)} \\
    pr_i^{\text{cum}}(t) &= \omega \cdot  pr_i^\text{price} + (1 - \omega) \cdot pr_i^\text{size}
\end{align*}

In the above, $Y_i(t)$ is the production of goods by firm $i$, $pr_i^\text{price}(t)$ represent the first (1) aspect of matching and $pr_i^{\text{size}}(t)$ the second (2). 

The last parameter represent the pass - through of import import prices in the model. It governs how sensitive are they to shock in foreign country. Originally the foreign prices are assumed to follow the domestic sectoral averages. Thus the import price for good $m: P_m(t)$ is determined by previous sectoral average ($\bar{P}_g(t - 1)$) adjusted for inflation $P_m(t) = \bar{P}_g(t - 1)(1 + \pi^e(t))$. With the introduced the pass - trough parameter it becomes:

\begin{equation}
    P_m(t) = \bar{P}_g(t - 1)( 1+ \pi^e(t) \cdot \bar{\pi} )
\end{equation}

With Bayesian methods we want to find:

\begin{equation}
    p(\theta \mid x_{\text{obs}}) = \frac{p(x_{\text{obs}} | \theta) p(\theta)}{p(x_{\text{obs}})}
\end{equation}

With $x_{\text{obs}}$ referring to the observed trajectory of five economic aggregates: real GDP, GDP deflator, real household consumption, real capital formation, real investment. However, the left hand side of the above expression is tractable and difficult to derive (Dyer et al. 2022, 2024). 

\subsubsection*{Prior selection}
The inference procedure, as described by Dyer et al. starts by choosing appropriate priors for the choose parameters. Following the modeling choice of Alves et al., and lack of strong signals to deviate, we decided to use uniform priors $(p(\theta))$ with bounds presented in table \ref{tab:parameters_to_estimate}, such that the original values are within the bounds and values are economically feasible. However, we adopted the approach to let the priors be quite wide and rely on prior checks and SBI procedures to capture the information in the posterior.

We investigate whether our chosen priors give rise to a plausible GDP trajectories by running Prior Predictive Checks. To avoid overfitting to a specific time period, run the checks for multiple calibration dates ($C = 5$), each time for 3 years. Thus if the resulting trajectories look similar to the observed one we keep the proposed prior. If we observed a unrealistic deviation (e.g. the GDP dips drastically or explode), we slightly restrict the prior and try again. The final priors are in table. (\ref{tab:parameters_to_estimate})

Moreover, for each parameter, a identification screen was run before training. We vary the parameter through the prior and plot the trajectories of the aggregates. If they move, we hope that NPE and NRE are able to identify them from the data. If the parameters do not influence the observables training is bound to return the prior, since data carry no information. 

\begin{enumerate}
    \item [$\omega$: ] Since its a weighting coefficient, we know $0 \leq w \leq1$. Thus adopting the aforementioned approach, we keep $\omega \sim \operatorname{Unif}(0, 1)$ which is centered around the original value.
    
    \item [$\lambda_p$: ] Since this parameter acts as temperature for softmax, it can technically be any positive real number. However, to keep the prior uninformative, bounded and centered around the original value, we decided to test: $\lambda_p \sim \operatorname{Unif}(0.001, 5)$.

    \item [$\bar{\pi}$: ] While the pass - through coefficient is typically below 1, it is possible to observe values higher than 1. Thus, to account for that and have the recovery value inside the bound we decide to use: $\operatorname{Unif}(0, 1.5)$
\end{enumerate}
Plots for prior predictive checks and identification sweeps can be found in the Appendix.

\subsubsection*{NPE}
Following Thiele (2025) we decided to use Neural Spline Flow architecture (NSF) for NPE inference. It is an extension of Normalizing Flows (NF) technique based on transforming some base (simple) distribution ($p_U$, usually a standard normal) into the target one ($p_X$), by a sequence of parametrized, invertible functions. It is uses the change of variable formula ($X = g(U) : g^{-1} = f$):
\begin{equation} \label{eq:nfs}
    p_X(x) = p_U(f(x)) \big| \det J_f (x) \big|
\end{equation}
Which can be unrolled to a composition (or flow) $f = f_n \circ \dots \circ f_2 \circ f_1$ where each $f_i $ is differentiable and invertible with $g_i = f^{-1}_i$. Thus by sampling from $U$ we can easily obtain $p_X(x)$. The functions $f_i$ are parametrized by $\phi$, and optimized by maximizing the data ($x_1, x_2, \dots, x_R \sim p_X$) likelihood under the model:

\begin{equation} \label{eq:amortized_NPE}
    \hat{\phi} = \arg \max_{\phi} \sum_{i = 1}^R \log q_\phi(x_i)
\end{equation}

NF modeling have two independent choices: the dependency structure and scalar transform. The first one specified how marginal variables of $x$ (given it is a vector) influence each other. Scalar transform dictates the way values are propagate through the network (see \eqref{eq:maf_regressive} below). The main approach used for NPE is based on Masked Autoregressive Flow (MAF) (Papamakarios et al. 2017). MAF mainly specifies the dependency structure, by enforcing autoregressive nature of the flow and is constructed using gaussian MADE (Germain et al. 2015) blocks as the flow functions ($f_i$). 

MADE is a NN that approximates a joint density: $q_\phi(X_1, \dots , X_n) $ by a series of conditionals: $q_\phi(\cdot) = \prod_i^n p(x_i | x_{j < i})$ (autoregressive). It uses binary masks to compute all conditionals in a single forward pass instead of coupled neural networks. For example, for a dense layer $h = Wx + b$, a MADE would introduce a mask matrix $W$, that restrict impossible connections e.g.: $p(x_2 | x_1)$ only depends on $p(x_1)$. Thus we have a MADE dense layer: $h = (M \odot W)x + b$. With $\odot$ being element wise multiplication. MADE networks can be used for density estimation, then to compute $p(x_1, \cdots, x_n)$, we draw $\{ u_1, \cdots, u_n \}$ from some simple predefined distribution and run the network forward. However, as MAF experiments suggests, stacking these blocks can improve density estimation. In that case we can use the resulting joint distribution from one MADE block as sampling distribution for $u$'s for the next ones. 

MAF models the conditional densities as gaussians: $p(x_i | x_{<i}) \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_i, \exp (\alpha_i))$, where $\mu_i$ and $\alpha_i$ depend on $x_{<i}$. Thus, given draws for $u_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ we can simulate (propagate) $x_i$ by: 
\begin{equation} \label{eq:maf_regressive}
    x_i = \mu_i + u_i \cdot \exp \alpha_i 
\end{equation}

The equation \eqref{eq:maf_regressive} is used to propagate both ways: sampling (draw $u$, obtain $x$) and density evaluation: given $x$ compute corresponding $u$ (invertibility). It is an example of an affine transform, and can be improved upon. Durkan et al. (2019) suggest to replace this step by rational - quadratic spline transform. Or more generally splines. Thus Neural Spline Flow (NSF) mainly specifies the second axis of NF modeling.

However, 

Regardless of the architecture chosen, the learning for sample $\{ \theta_i, x_i \}_{i = 1}^N$ proceeds with minimizing the cross entropy (equivalent to minimizing KL divergence between true posterior: $p(\theta \mid x)$ and learned posterior: $q_\phi(\theta \mid x)$). One usually uses standard deep learning techniques such stochastic gradient descent with optimizers such as ADAM (e.g. Dyer et al. 2024) to update the parameters $\phi$, with loss given by:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:nnl}
    \mathcal{L}(\phi) = -\frac{1}{N}\sum_i^N \log \tilde{q}_\phi(\theta_i \mid x_i)
\end{equation}
For the SNPE\_C transformed posterior:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:apt}
    \tilde{q}_\phi(\theta \mid x) = q_\phi(\theta \mid x)\frac{\tilde{p}(\theta)}{p(\theta)} \frac{1}{Z}
\end{equation}


\subsubsection*{Embedding strategies} \label{sec: embedding_nets}
Next step is to choose an embedding method. Both methods: (S)NPE and (S)NRE can work on a set of handcrafted statistics and embedding networks. The first methods comes from research for standard ABC approaches. While the latter comes from NPE and NRE use of gradient learning, which allow for training of additional embedding nets. Moreover, such networks can allow for more structured learning, exploiting relationships between data points / dimensions. It has been found that NNs can greatly improve posterior estimation and parameter recovery (e.g. Alves et al. 2026). Thus, we focus on the neural embeddings, but keep one trained on hand - crafted statistics for reference. Out single input $x_\text{in}$ has a dimension: $(C, Q, T)$ as described in the data section.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item [a. ] Hand - crafted statistics: $s(x_i) : \mathbb{R}^{T_\text{train}}\to \mathbb{R}^d$. With $D = 10$ being the number of statistics. We use  summaries chosen by Dyer et al. (2024) and ALves et al. (2026): mean, variance, max, min, $q_p$, $\gamma_k$. For $p \in \{ .25, .5, .75 \}$ and $k \in \{ 1, 2, 3 \}$, compute for each feature of the output. For each input $x_\text{in}$ we extract the last two dimension, and treat runs with different calibration dates as additional data points and assign them the same prior draws (under the assumption that parameters remain constant throughout the calibration window, they constitute a valid draw from the joint distribution) using PyTorch function: repeat\_interleave$(\cdot)$ (PyTorch: Paszke et al. 2017). Resulting in $x_\text{in}$ of shape: $(Q, D)$ and the sample size $N_\text{samples}$ grows to $N_\text{samples}  \cdot C = 5 \cdot 10^5$.
 
    \item [b. ] Use a embedding network to learn the features. Here we compare a few architectures based on GRU RNN architecture proposed by Cho et al. (2014)
    \begin{enumerate}

        \item [i. ] The basic RNN over features. Similarly as for the handcrafted statistic case, we unroll $x_\text{in}$ along calibration dates to obtain more sample data points ($5 \cdot 10^5$). For this network we use PyTorch implementation of GRU RNN network with two hidden layers of size $H = 64$ to process the tensor: $x_\text{in}$ of shape $(Q, T)$. The block is followed by one MLP layer with output size of $16$. The MLP takes the final hidden state from the RNN block.
        
        \item [ii. ] Simple Hierarchical RNN over features. It takes original $x_\text{in}$ with different calibration dates. We use the same GRU RNN as in (i) over the last two dimensions. We take the last hidden value from the RNN, what results in a tensor: $h_\text{out}$ of shape $(C, H)$ and compute the mean over calibration dates: $h^\text{mean}_j = \operatorname{Mean}_{1 \leq i \leq C}\left( h^\text{out}_{i, j} \right)$ for $j = 1, \dots, H$ and feed the resulting vector: $h^\text{mean} \in \mathbb{R}^H$ through a single dense layer with output dimension of 16. 
        
        \item [iii.] Proper Hierarchical RNN over features and calibration dates. This network uses two GRU RNN blocks, each with two layers. First over the latter two dimensions with hidden size of $H_1 = 64$ what yields a tensor of shape: $(C, H_1)$. Which we then pass through the second block with hidden size of $H_2 = 32$. The last hidden size of the second RNN is passed through a single layer MLP with output dimension of 16. 
    \end{enumerate}
    
\end{enumerate}

We used the same generated dateset for all architectures, thus technically the basic RNN has more data points (5 times more), but since they are expanded with the same parameter draw, we test whether more structured embedding network can recover the parameters more precisely. 

\subsubsection*{Sampling}
The posteriors obtained via NPE and NRE are approximate and require different techniques to obtain samples from. For methods such as NPE, which model the conditional distribution directly, sampling does not require normalisation. Thus, in most cases one can rely on simple Rejection Sampling. However, for bounded priors where posterior mass is concentrated some draws can leak. Thus, one needs to use more stable, but slower algorithms such as MCMC Metropolis-Hastings. For NRE, the posterior is not available directly, but we can recover the unnormalized density. Many methods can be used, for this sampling problem with the most common one being based on MCMC e.g. Metropolis Hastings.


\subsubsection*{Training}
With the given priors, we train multiple NPE and NRE estimators according to Algorithm 1 in Dyer et al. (2024). We perform single round inference with $N_\text{samples} = 1000$ as outlined below:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Draw $N_\text{samples}$ samples from the prior : $\{ \theta_i \}_i^N$
    
    \item Perform NPE / NRE estimation on pre generated dataset for a given embedding (see section \ref{sec: embedding_nets})
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item [NPE: ] Train until convergence, with Stochastic Gradient Descent on the loss function from NPE\_C
        \item [NRE: ] Train until convergence: sample a mini batch from all generated so far samples, and update the parameters on the basis of finite - sample multinomial logistic loss (Durkan NRE: see eq: \eqref{eq:multinomial_loss})
    \end{enumerate}
    
    \item Update the prior:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item [NPE: ] Set the new prior directly: $\tilde{p}(\theta) = q_\phi(\theta \mid x_\text{obs})$
        \item [NRE: ] Set unnormalized prior: $\tilde{p}(\theta) \propto \exp \{ s_\phi(\theta, x_\text{obs}) \}$
    \end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Validation of the posteriors}
As with other Bayesian Methods, evaluating the validity of the posterior is a crucial step in the inference process. We condition on coupled observed trajectories $x_\text{obs}$ starting from 2010Q1, till 2016Q4 (the $x_\text{obs}$ has to be the same shape as training data); for the single layer RNN and hand drafted statistics we condition on the last observeed series spanning: 2014Q1 - 2016Q4. We use standard rejection sampling to obtain draws from the conditional posterior for NPE evaluation and MCMC algorithm for NRE posterior. The first test is merely visual diagnostics, while the second (sweep) provides identification test. Ideally, all parameters are identified from the data, which would be the case if the posteriors collapse onto a smaller support, with possibly multiple modes. The last one (SBC) given a formal statistical test for NPE procedure and validity of the final posterior. It is important to note, that our two important checks: sweep and SBC rely on the fact that the posterior is amortized. That is approximated well on the whole support, and thus the choice for single round inference.
\begin{enumerate}

    \item Recovery test for baseline values - we generate data from the baseline model and run inference on those new data points to obtain $N$  posterior samples: $\theta_i$. We check whether the ground truth value lies inside the 95\% confidence interval. 

    \item Parameter identification / recovery sweep - assess whether the posterior recovers (identifies) the parameters: (for each parameter separately)
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item [i. ] For each of the 3 parameters we take 7 values evenly spaced between the prior bounds ($\tilde{\theta} \in \mathbb{R}^{7 \times 3}$ ) and define $\theta_\text{base} = (\frac{1}{2}, \frac{5}{2}, \frac{3}{4})$ as the mean of priors. 
        \item [ii. ] For each parameter, we take corresponding values of $\tilde{\theta}_i \in \mathbb{R}^7$, and run simulation for each value of $\tilde{\theta}_i$ with the other two parameters according to $\theta_\text{base}$ thus $\{x_i \}_{i =1}^M \sim p(x|\theta_\text{base} \land \tilde{\theta})$
        \item [iii. ] For each simulator run $\{ x_i \}$ we draw $N = 1000$ posterior samples: $\{ \theta^{(i)}_j \} \sim q_\phi(\theta \mid x_i)$, thus for each parameter combination (7 per dim $\rightarrow $ 21 total) we obtain a set of $\theta$'s of dim: $(N, M)$.
        \item [iv. ] Compute the location statistic across posterior draws. We took the median (giving a median's distribution across simulator run):
        \begin{equation}
            m_i = \operatorname{Median}_{1 \leq j \leq N} \left( \theta^{(i)}_j \right)
        \end{equation}
        We average the medians across simulation runs: $\bar{m} = \operatorname{Mean}_{1 \leq i \leq M}\left( m_i \right)$
        And compute average's  90\% credible interval ($I = q_{.95} - q_{.05}$) using quantiles from the median's distribution:
        \begin{equation}
            q_\alpha= \operatorname{Quantile}^\alpha_{1 \leq i \leq M} \left(m_i  \right)
        \end{equation}
        
        \item [v. ] Plot the points: $\{ \tilde{\theta},  \bar{m}\}$ for the given parameter. Together with the credible bounds. We add the line $y = x$ as reference for a perfect identification.
    \end{enumerate}
    After the procedure we obtain $d = 3$ plots
    
    \item Simulation Based Calibration - the formal diagnostics procedure as described by Talts et al. (2018). It is important to note that SBC only checks a necessary condition, meaning it does not prove that the calibration procedure was correct. It can only detect whether posterior is wrong, e.g. a posterior equal to the prior passes the test. Moreover, is only valid for amortized posteriors, since it samples from entire prior. We follow Algorithm 1 from that paper, with $L = 1000$ posterior samples, and repeated for $N = 1000$ prior draws (with $B = 20$ histogram bins):
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item [i. ] Draw a prior sample: $\tilde{{\theta}} \sim p(\theta)$
        \item [ii. ] Draw a simulated data set: $\tilde{x} \sim p(x \mid \theta)$ 
        \item [iii. ] Draw posterior samples: $\{ \theta_1, \dots , \theta_L \} \sim q_\phi(\theta | \tilde{x})$
        \item [iv. ] For each one - dimensional random variable do:
        \begin{enumerate}
            \item Compute the rank statistic as in \eqref{eq:rank_statistics} 
            \item Update the histogram with the rank statistic
        \end{enumerate}
    \end{enumerate}
    We complement the histogram with 99\% band expected under a uniform histogram. It represents distance between 0.005 and 0.995 percentiles of the $\operatorname{Binomial}(N, (L + 1)^{-1})$ distribution to help identify anomalies (we expect one bin out of a hundred to be outside of the band). Moreover, we plot the empirical CDF of the bins distribution: $\text{bin\_rank}_j = \lfloor r_j / B \rfloor $ for $j = 1, \dots, N$. The histogram check allows for detecting structural inconsistencies or biases in the posterior estimation. When needed, we also perform coverage approximations\footnote{For each prior draw $\{\tilde{\theta}_j\}_{j = 1}^N$ and corresponding $L$ posterior samples we compute 90\% and 95\% CI out quantiles from $L$ draws store the results: $\tilde{\theta}_i \in \text{CI}$ for both intervals. We average the binary results across $N$ prior samples. For an ideal procedure, we would like to have the mean to be equal corresponding confidence level.} and compute z - scores \footnote{Since rank $\{r_j\}_{j = 1}^N \in \operatorname{DU}[0, L] \implies \mu_r = \frac{L}{2} = 500, \sigma_r = \sqrt{\frac{L^2  - 1}{12}} \approx 289$. The mean rank follows approximately $\bar{r} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_r, \sigma = \frac{\sigma_r}{\sqrt{N}})$ for N prior draws by CLT. Thus the z - score can be found $z_{\bar{r}} = \left(\frac{\bar{r} - 500}{289}\right) \cdot \sqrt{1000}$ } for the mean rank based on the central limit theorem for each parameter.
    
    \item Posterior Predictive Check - run the simulation $N$ times with parameter samples from the conditional posterior, check whether the resultant trajectories look sensible as compared to the observed trajectory: $x_\text{obs}$
    
\end{enumerate}

\begin{equation} \label{eq:rank_statistics}
    r\left( \{f(\theta_i)\}^L_i, f(\tilde{\theta}) \right) = \sum_{i = 1}^L\mathbb{I} \left[f(\theta_i) < f(\tilde{\theta}) \right] \in [0, L]
\end{equation}

\subsection{Forecast Evaluation} \label{sec:methodology_forecast}
We compare the out-of sample forecasting performance of the methods by computing a median of the root mean squared forecast error out of 100 MC runs with parameter values taken to be a sample mean (of $N = 1000$ samples). The medians is computed for each observable separately for various forecast lengths. We follow Wiese et al. (2024) and report the RSFME medians for forecasts of lengths: $T \in \{ 1, 2, 4, 8, 12 \}$ in quarters. The forecast begins from $T_\text{begin} = 2017Q1$ with maximal forecast horizon of 12 quarters (the standard forecast horizon used in both referenced paper) thus we have: $T_\text{end} = 2019Q4$ (inclusive). 

\begin{equation}
    \left\{ \text{RMSFE}_T \right\}  = \operatorname{Median}_{1 \leq i \leq 1000} \sqrt{ \frac{1}{T - T_\text{begin}} \sum_{t = T_\text{begin} + 1}^T \left( \hat{X}_i(t) - Y(t) \right)^2}
\end{equation}

Where $\hat{X}_i(t)$ represents the forecasted value at time $t$ for a given observable by run $i$ and $Y(t)$ the observed value for this observable.
